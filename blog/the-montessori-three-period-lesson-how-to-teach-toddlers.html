The Montessori Three-Period Lesson: How to Teach Toddlers New Skills at Home

Learn the Montessori three-period lesson and how to teach toddlers new skills at home, with step-by-step scripts and practical guidance.

By BoonHouse Publishing · 2026-09-30

Beatriz sat at the kitchen table on a Tuesday morning with three colored blocks arranged in a row in front of her 3-year-old. She named the red block six times in different voices, with different gestures, with growing enthusiasm. When she asked "Can you show me red?" her daughter pointed to the yellow one. By the fourth attempt, she was starting to think the problem was her.

The problem was the sequence.

The Montessori three-period lesson is a structured method for teaching toddlers new vocabulary, concepts, and skills through three distinct cognitive stages. Once you understand what each stage does and why they must stay separate, the method works reliably at home with materials as simple as colored blocks or smooth and rough stones.

What Is the Montessori Three-Period Lesson?

The method traces back to Édouard Séguin, a French physician who developed a three-stage teaching sequence in the 1840s to work with children who had intellectual disabilities. Maria Montessori studied his approach and brought it into early childhood education in the early 1900s, where it became the backbone of vocabulary instruction in Montessori classrooms worldwide.

The cognitive logic is grounded in how the brain consolidates new information: first through passive intake, then through active recognition, then through independent recall. Each phase places different demands on memory and language production. Developmental research places the average working memory span for a 3-year-old at two to three items per session. Mixing phases, or pushing to recall before recognition is solid, overloads that capacity, which is why the pointing goes wrong.

The three periods, in order:

Period One: This is. You introduce the concept with a plain, neutral statement. "This is the triangle." "This is rough." "This is blue." No questions, no testing, no elaboration. One name, one object, repeated calmly two or three times. The child's only job in this period is to receive.

Period Two: Show me. You ask the child to act on the concept without requiring them to produce the word themselves. "Show me the triangle." "Touch the rough one." "Can you put the blue block here?" This is where consolidation happens. The child demonstrates understanding without the pressure of verbal recall. When they point to the wrong item, name the correct one once, hold it up briefly, set it down, and ask again immediately. No correction, no re-teaching, no visible disappointment.

Period Three: What is this? Now you ask for recall. "What is this?" "What do we call this one?" Period Three reveals what the previous two periods built. A child who hesitates or says nothing is communicating that Period Two needs more time. The lesson returns there and tries again a day or two later.

How Do You Teach Toddlers New Skills at Home With the Three Periods?

The structure works beyond vocabulary. Montessori guides apply the three-period lesson to any skill with discrete, nameable parts: colors, shapes, letters, early math concepts like "more" and "fewer," practical life actions like "pouring" and "folding," and emotional vocabulary like "frustrated" or "disappointed." Whatever your child is working on right now, the sequence stays the same: name it without asking for anything back, ask for recognition before you ask for recall.

Practical decisions shape how well the lesson lands at home. Start with two concepts per session, not six. Two done well produce better retention than eight done quickly. When Period Two is solid on "red" and "blue," you add "yellow." For children under three, concrete objects work better than flashcards: a smooth stone and a rough stone teach those words in a way a worksheet cannot, because the child can hold and compare them during Period Two.

Period One language should be minimal: one short phrase per object. "This is rough." If you add "can you feel how different it is from the smooth one?" the extra language competes with the word you are trying to anchor. Matching the session to the right moment matters equally. Five minutes of genuine focus before lunch works better than twenty minutes during a transition. The lesson is short by design.

What to Say at Each Stage When Teaching Toddlers New Concepts?

Here are working scripts for each period, with fallbacks for the moments that stall.

For Period One, the script is intentional in its simplicity. Hold up the object and say its name: "This is the circle." Set it down. Pick it up. "This is the circle." Set it down once more. "This is the circle." Three statements, no questions. Move to the second concept and do the same. Then stop the session.

Period Two carries the most variation and the most value. Set both objects on the table. "Show me the circle." If the child points correctly, say "yes" and nothing more. Ask for the other: "Show me the square." Keep alternating, varying the phrasing: "Can you put the circle on the mat?" "Which one is the circle? Touch it." If the child points to the wrong one, hold up the correct one, say its name once, set it down, and immediately ask again. Keep the tone flat and patient throughout.

If Period Two stalls, the response is the same every time. End the session: "We'll look at these again tomorrow." Put the objects away without commentary. A child who loses interest or seems genuinely uncertain after three or four prompts is telling you the concept needs another day. More repetition inside the same sitting produces confusion, not consolidation.

Period Three should arrive only when Period Two is solid. Hold up one object. "What is this?" Wait without filling the silence. If the child names it correctly, move to the second. If they say nothing, smile and say "That's all right, we'll look at it again" and return to Period Two the next day. The child who goes quiet in Period Three is telling you the word has not consolidated yet. That is useful information. The lesson continues at the pace that works.

The Honest Cost

The three-period lesson is slower than it looks. Period Two for a single concept often takes three or four separate sessions across multiple days before a child is ready for Period Three. That is the method working as designed.

The other cost is attention. The lesson works best with one adult and one child, with a few minutes of real focus. It does not run well as a group activity with mixed-age siblings, and it does not work as a background task during meals or transitions. Carving out that kind of dedicated window is genuinely harder than most parenting advice acknowledges. Three or four short sessions per week is enough to make progress. The lesson just takes longer to arrive at Period Three.

The payoff is proportional. Children who move through all three periods for a concept retain the word or skill more reliably and transfer it to new contexts more quickly than children taught through repetition alone. The reason is structural: they built the concept during a low-pressure recognition phase, rather than drilling the word under the pressure of being asked to produce it before it was ready.

A week after her first attempt, Beatriz set out two blocks instead of three. She stayed in Period Two for four full minutes, asking "Show me the blue one" in different ways, before she moved to Period Three at all. When her daughter pointed to the correct block and said "blue," Beatriz said "yes" and moved on. She had stopped expecting the lesson to move as fast as her worry did.

FAQ

At what age can toddlers begin the Montessori three-period lesson?

Most Montessori practitioners introduce a simplified version of Period One and Period Two with children between 18 months and 2 years, using only two concepts per session and very concrete objects. Period Three recall is typically saved for closer to age 3, when verbal production is more reliable. A useful readiness signal is whether the child can consistently follow a simple two-word instruction like "bring cup" or "touch nose."

How long should each period take when teaching new skills at home?

Period One takes two to three minutes for two or three concepts. Period Two is the most variable: a single session might run three to five minutes, but full consolidation can span several sessions over multiple days, depending on how new the concept is for the child. Period Three is typically the shortest phase once it arrives, often under two minutes. Total active lesson time per session rarely needs to exceed ten minutes for toddlers, and stopping before the child shows fatigue produces better retention than pressing past the natural end of their focus.

Can the three-period lesson be used for skills beyond vocabulary?

Yes, and Montessori guides use it broadly. The same structure applies to practical life skills like pouring or folding napkins, to early math concepts like "more" and "fewer," to emotional vocabulary like "worried" or "calm," and to pre-reading readiness with letter sounds. The method works for any skill with distinct, nameable steps: name it first with no expectation of a response, ask for recognition before recall, and hold Period Three until recognition is solid.
