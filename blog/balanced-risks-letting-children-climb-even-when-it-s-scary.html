Balanced Risks: Letting Children Climb, Even When It's Scary

Should I let my kid climb trees and take risks? Discover how allowing supervised, age-appropriate physical challenges builds confidence and resilience.

By BoonHouse Publishing

Naledi watched her five-year-old son, Leo, scale the old oak tree in their backyard. His small hands gripped the rough bark, his sneakers finding purchase on knots and low branches. It was 4:30 in the afternoon, the sun casting long shadows, and the familiar urge to call out, "Be careful, that's too high!" tightened in her throat.

That visceral instinct to protect our children from physical pain is powerful. When a child climbs a little too high on the playground structures, or attempts a daring leap, our stomachs drop. Words like, "Get down from there." can escape before conscious thought. This desire to shield children from harm is natural. However, when we try to eliminate all physical risk from childhood experiences, we also remove a primary way children learn about their own bodies and boundaries. This can lead to children who are less confident in their physical abilities and more hesitant to explore their world independently.

Allowing age-appropriate, supervised challenges offers many benefits. Children gain self-confidence, improve spatial awareness, and develop problem-solving abilities. These experiences teach them about gravity, balance, and their own strength, lessons that cannot be fully learned from the ground. Norwegian researcher Ellen Sandseter of Queen Maud University College, whose 2011 paper in Evolutionary Psychology found that children who are allowed supervised risky play, like climbing and balancing at height, develop better risk assessment and show fewer anxiety-related fears later on. This research confirms what many parents observe: children learn through doing, and part of that learning involves encountering and overcoming physical obstacles, which builds genuine resilience.

This isn't about encouraging recklessness; it is about providing opportunities for children to learn within controlled environments. A low branch on a sturdy tree, a slightly uneven log, or a rock scramble in a park all present chances for children to test their strength, balance, and coordination. These experiences are invaluable for developing motor skills and a realistic understanding of cause and effect, which are important for safety in the long run.

The Trouble with "Be Careful"

The phrase "Be careful" is often vague and unhelpful. To a child, it rarely offers actionable information; it simply communicates an underlying sense of adult fear and anxiety. This can unintentionally teach them to distrust their own physical capabilities, making them overly cautious even in safe situations. When children are given the chance to engage with calculated physical challenges, like climbing a tree or balancing on a log, they learn to assess potential dangers independently. They begin to understand their own limits, how to push them safely, and when to retreat, leading to a deeper understanding of their surroundings.

Shifting to Mindful Support

Instead of stopping the play or shouting warnings, parents can adjust their physical presence and verbal guidance. This approach supports a child's exploration while still offering a safety net.

  • Get Close, Stay Quiet: Position yourself near enough to intervene if a fall occurs, but give your child the freedom to try without your voice interrupting their concentration. Your presence alone can be reassuring, signaling that you trust their abilities even as you stand ready to assist.
  • Ask Awareness Questions: Rather than saying, "Don't fall," ask questions that prompt self-assessment. Try, "What is your next step?" or "Do you feel stable on that branch?" This encourages them to think through their movements and consider the consequences of each choice, rather than relying solely on an adult's instruction.
  • Focus on Strategy: Guide their attention to specific actions. For example, "Look with your eyes to find a secure place for your foot," or "Can you test that branch before putting your full weight on it?" These specific prompts are much more informative than a general warning, helping them develop their own internal safety compass.

When a child successfully overcomes a physical challenge on their own, the boost to their resilience and self-trust is considerable. These moments build a foundation of self-reliance that extends far beyond the playground, preparing them for future challenges with a sense of capability and courage. By stepping back and offering mindful support, we allow our children the profound satisfaction of mastering their world, one climb at a time.
