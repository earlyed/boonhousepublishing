Why Toddlers Have Tantrums: Beyond Manipulation to Emotional Overwhelm

Understand why toddlers have tantrums, moving past ideas of manipulation to recognize emotional overwhelm. Learn how to respond with calm presence.

By BoonHouse Publishing

At 5 o’clock, the witching hour for many families, Ingrid watched her 3-year-old son, Leo, collapse onto the kitchen floor in a heap of tears. The cause: she had handed him the blue cup instead of the red one. The sound of his wails echoed through the house, and in that moment, it certainly felt like he was doing it on purpose, a calculated move to get his way.

It is a common thought heard in grocery stores and playgrounds everywhere: "They are just doing that to get attention," or "Don't give in, they are trying to manipulate you." When a toddler throws themselves onto the floor because of a minor disappointment, the intensity can make it seem intentional.

The Brain Under Stress

Viewing a tantrum as a calculated strategy misinterprets how a child's brain actually develops. A toddler's prefrontal cortex, the area of the brain responsible for logic, reasoning, self-control, and emotional regulation, is highly immature. This critical part of the brain will not be fully developed until well into their twenties, as revealed by child psychiatrist Jay Giedd's 1999 longitudinal MRI study at the National Institute of Mental Health, which tracked the same children's brains over time and found the prefrontal cortex, the seat of impulse control, is still not fully wired until the mid-twenties.

When a young child experiences disappointment, their primitive, emotional brain, specifically the amygdala, takes over completely. They are quite literally experiencing a neurological fight or flight response. In these moments, a child is not purposefully trying to be difficult. They are genuinely overwhelmed and struggling with big feelings their developing brain cannot yet manage.

Being the Anchor

Once a child is experiencing a full emotional blowout, reasoning, lecturing, or shouting will not work. Their brain cannot process logic in that state. Your primary goal is simply to be their emotional anchor, a steady presence in their storm.

Stay Present

When a child's emotions escalate, their world can feel chaotic and unsafe. Your physical presence, even without direct touch, communicates safety. Ensure they are not in harm's way, perhaps by moving dangerous objects or guiding them to a safer space on the floor. If they push you away, respect that boundary while remaining nearby, a quiet, reassuring presence. This teaches them that you are a reliable source of security, even when they feel out of control.

Lower Your Voice

The human nervous system is highly attuned to others, especially between a child and their caregiver. When you maintain a quiet, even tone, you offer a physiological anchor. Loud voices or frantic tones will only intensify their stress response. Instead, consciously slow your breathing and speak in a low, steady voice. This act of co-regulation helps their overwhelmed brain begin to downshift from its fight or flight state, slowly bringing their own nervous system back into balance.

Validate, Don't Fix

Validation is not agreement; it is acknowledgement. You do not need to concede to their demand to soothe their feelings. If the boundary stands, affirm it gently. The goal is to name the emotion they are experiencing. Phrases like, "You are so frustrated that you can't have the red cup right now," or "It looks like you feel really sad about this," help a child connect their intense physical sensations to an emotion. This practice builds emotional literacy, teaching them that all feelings are acceptable, even if all behaviors are not. It also shows them that their internal experience is seen and understood by you, strengthening your bond.

When the storm passes, they will learn a profound lesson: their biggest, ugliest emotions are not enough to scare you away or make you love them less. This consistent, calm response builds a foundation of trust and emotional resilience that will serve them well into adulthood.
