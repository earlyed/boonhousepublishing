What to Do When Another Child Is Mean to Your Toddler

When another child is mean to your toddler, here is what to do in the moment, what to say, and how to help your child feel confident again.

By BoonHouse Publishing · 2024-12-21

Leila was on the bench at the playground when the other girl took the shovel straight out of her son's hand. Ezra was two years and eight months old, not yet able to say "give that back," and he stood open-mouthed for three full seconds before the crying started. Leila felt the heat rise in her chest. She did not know whether to cross the sandbox or wait.

When another child is mean to your toddler, three things work: move in close, name what happened, and give your child the words. The sequence matters less than the combination. All three, in whatever order the moment allows, are what your toddler needs in the next sixty seconds.

This situation finds almost every parent of a child between ages two and four, and the instinctive responses are often the ones that leave a toddler least prepared for the next time. Scooping them up and leaving is comfort, not preparation. Telling the other child "that wasn't nice" is boundary-setting aimed at the wrong person. Your child needs to be regulated first, then named, then equipped. That is the order.

Why Toddlers Can Be Cruel (and Why It Doesn't Mean What You Fear)

The grabbing, the pushing, the exclusion: none of it is malice in children under three. Malice requires a theory of mind, the ability to hold in your head that another person has wants, feelings, and a perspective distinct from your own. Developmental research, including studies from the University of Washington published in 2013, shows that theory of mind consolidates reliably between ages three and five. A two-year-old who takes your child's bucket is working from a brain that cannot yet hold "this belongs to someone who wants it back."

This matters because it changes what you do. You are not facing a child who chose to be cruel. You are facing a developmental mismatch: your toddler's pain is real, and the other child's intent was not malicious. Both things are true at once. What your toddler needs from you is not justice. What they need is a name for what just happened and evidence that an adult noticed.

The other thing this understanding offers is permission to stay calm. A parent who says "she took your shovel and that wasn't okay" without escalating is already doing the hardest part. Your toddler is watching your register as much as they are experiencing the incident, and how alarmed you are tells them how alarmed to be.

What Do You Say to Your Toddler When Another Child Is Mean?

Go to them first. Crouch down. Make it physical before verbal. A hand on the back, eye contact, your body close. Then name what happened in plain language.

"She took your shovel. That hurt your feelings."

Not "it's okay." To your toddler right now, it is not okay. Not "don't cry." They should cry. Something real happened to them. The naming sentence is a mirror: what you saw, what it probably felt like. Five to eight words. Then wait.

Once the first wave of crying slows, give them the words they do not yet have:

"You can say: I was using that."

One sentence. Repeat it once if they are still composing themselves. Then: "If she doesn't give it back, come find me." This is the complete scaffolding for a child under three: name the feeling, offer the script, promise backup. They cannot do all three on their own yet, and they are not supposed to. That is two-year-old neurology, not a flaw in your child.

If your toddler is too flooded to absorb any of this, hold the language until their breathing slows. Hold them, say "I'm right here," and wait. The words come after the body calms, never before. A flooded nervous system cannot encode new language, and the sequence that works is regulate, then name, then equip.

For families who want to practice this before the next encounter, What to Do when Someone Is Mean walks toddlers through the same three steps in a calm, illustrated Montessori format: feel it, say it, get help. Reading it at home plants the language before the child needs it under pressure.

Does It Help to Talk to the Other Child's Parent?

Sometimes, and the answer depends on the pattern.

If the incident was a single grab or a shove on a crowded playground, skip the conversation. These are ordinary developmental collisions. A conversation about a one-off tends to produce defensiveness in the other parent rather than anything resembling repair for your child.

If the same child has been unkind to yours across multiple visits, a quiet, factual word is worth having. The framing that tends to land: "I've noticed your daughter and Ezra have had some hard moments together. I'm not sure who starts what, but I wanted to flag it so we can both keep an eye on it." That is not an accusation. It is a co-observation, and most parents receive it as such.

What does not help: waiting for the other parent to produce a genuine apology from their child in the moment. Coerced apologies teach children to perform remorse, not feel it. The better outcome from a parent-to-parent conversation is awareness and closer supervision, not a scene on the playground.

If your toddler is also working out that they do not need every peer to warm to them, I Don't Need Everyone to Like Me addresses exactly that: some friendships click and some do not, and a child who is cool toward yours says nothing about what your child is worth.

The Part That Nobody Says Out Loud

Parents often want to prevent their toddler from experiencing peer unkindness at all. This is love. The attempt, in the form of constant close supervision, immediate removal at the first sign of friction, or lengthy processing conversations after every incident, can take away the low-stakes social repetitions a toddler needs before primary school. Children build tolerance for conflict through small exposures, with an adult close enough to buffer but not so close that they step in before the child has had two seconds to try.

The honest cost of the approach in this post: you will get the timing wrong sometimes. You will scoop when you should have waited. You will wait when you should have moved in. That is not a failure of the method. They are learning from your reaction as much as from the incident itself, and what they are absorbing is whether an adult showed up, named what happened, and helped them carry it. The calibration improves with time. The showing up is the part that transfers.

If conflict at home extends to disagreements between your toddler and a sibling, or between your child and you, It's Okay to Not Agree gives young children their earliest vocabulary for a world where people want different things. That vocabulary moves with them to the playground.

Two days later, Leila was back at the same park. Ezra walked up to the sandbox and stopped at the edge. He looked back at her once. She nodded. He went in.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I teach my toddler to hit back if another child hits them?

Teaching a toddler to hit back solves the immediate moment and creates problems at every age after. The more durable sequence is to say "stop" in a loud, clear voice and then find an adult. That works at two, at five, and at twelve. Hitting back works at two and, by primary school, tends to mark your child as the aggressor in the adult version of events regardless of who started it. Teach the sequence, not the reflex.

My toddler says another child at daycare is always mean to them. What should I do?

Talk to the caregiver before drawing conclusions. Ask what they are observing and whether the pattern is documented. Good caregivers track these dynamics and will have information you do not. If the caregiver confirms a repeated pattern that is not improving, ask what the center's response plan looks like. Escalating a documented pattern is not overreacting. Treating a single report from a two-year-old as confirmed fact before checking with the adult in the room is.

How do I know if my toddler is being bullied or just experiencing normal conflict?

Bullying has three markers: repetition, a power imbalance, and deliberate intent to harm. A two-year-old grabbing a shovel once is normal conflict. Watch for avoidance: if your toddler stops wanting to go to the park or playgroup where the other child is, that is the clearest signal something warrants attention. One bad day is a bad day. Consistent avoidance of a specific place or person is a pattern, and patterns warrant a direct conversation with whoever supervises the space.
