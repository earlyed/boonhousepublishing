Positive Phrasing: What to Say Instead of 'Don't' to Your Toddler

Discover how to effectively communicate with your toddler by learning what to say instead of 'don't', promoting cooperation through clear and positive phrasing.

By BoonHouse Publishing

Devon watched her 3-year-old, Leo, race through the living room, a half-eaten banana clutched in his hand. Her heart pounded a little faster as he veered towards the antique lamp. 'Don't run, Leo!' she called out, but his momentum carried him forward, his eyes still fixed on the television. It felt like a constant battle, a loop of prohibitions that never quite landed.

Many parents find themselves in Devon's shoes, using words like 'Don't,' 'Stop,' or 'No' countless times a day. While these phrases are instinctive when we see a behavior we want to halt immediately, they often create an extra cognitive step for a young child. The developing brain processes language literally and visually. When you say, 'Don't run,' a child's brain first visualizes the act of running, and then it has to actively work backwards to figure out what they should be doing instead. This mental double-take is inefficient for a brain still building its inhibitory control.

Harvard psychologist Daniel Wegner's 1987 studies on thought suppression, the well-known 'white bear' experiments, found that telling someone what not to think about makes the forbidden thought harder to shake, not easier. The same tax on a fully developed adult brain lands even harder on a toddler's, which is still building the wiring to inhibit an action at all. Understanding this cognitive process helps parents move beyond simply reacting to unwanted behaviors. It encourages a proactive approach to communication, one that anticipates a child's need for direct instruction. Instead of expecting a young child to infer the correct action from a negative command, we can offer them the positive alternative upfront. This strategy respects their developmental stage and sets them up for success. Positive phrasing eliminates this confusion by telling the child exactly what action to take right from the start.

The Power of Clarity

Focusing on the desired behavior gives your child a clear roadmap for success. This approach reduces power struggles, which often arise from a child's frustration with unclear boundaries or a feeling of being constantly told 'no.' Instead, positive phrasing feels like helpful guidance, not an attack on their growing sense of self or their drive to explore. When children understand what they are expected to do, they are more likely to cooperate willingly, rather than simply complying out of fear or confusion. This clarity builds a foundation of trust and mutual respect in your daily interactions. It also helps children develop better self-regulation over time, as they learn to internalize expectations and make appropriate choices themselves, rather than relying solely on external commands.

Easy Everyday Script Swaps

Changing verbal habits takes conscious effort, but the immediate shift in your child's cooperation makes it worthwhile. Here are some common situations and how to reframe your language:

Instead of: 'Don't run in the house.' Try: 'Use your walking feet inside.'

Instead of: 'Stop screaming.' Try: 'Please use your quiet indoor voice.'

Instead of: 'Don't throw those blocks.' Try: 'Blocks stay on the floor. If you want to throw, here is a soft ball.'

Instead of: 'Stop spilling your milk.' Try: 'Keep your cup flat on the table, please.'

It takes practice to rewrite your own verbal habits, especially when reacting in the moment. Be patient with yourself as you make this shift. You will quickly notice that children cooperate much faster when they are given a clear command to follow rather than a restriction to decipher. This simple shift can make a significant difference in the rhythm of your home, changing moments of potential conflict into opportunities for teaching and connection. Clear communication leads to calmer days for everyone, building a more cooperative and understanding family environment.
