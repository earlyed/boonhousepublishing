How to Prepare Your Toddler for a New Sibling

How to prepare your toddler for a new sibling: timing, language, and Montessori-grounded scripts that help a toddler feel steady through the change.

By BoonHouse Publishing · 2025-06-23

Preparing a toddler for a new sibling means giving a child between 18 months and 4 years enough accurate information, language, and role to make sense of what is coming before it arrives. The goal is predictability. Toddlers do not panic because change is hard; they panic because they had no picture of the change before it hit them. When the picture is clear and the child has a part to play, much of the chaos parents dread either shrinks or never appears.

The work of preparation operates through three channels: information about what is happening and why, language the child can use when feelings spike, and a concrete role in the sibling relationship that gives the child something to move toward rather than something to resist. None of these channels requires a perfect parent. All three require starting early enough that the child has time to rehearse the new reality before it becomes the actual one.

When Is the Right Time to Prepare Your Toddler for a New Sibling?

The right window for most toddlers is 12 to 16 weeks before the due date. Start earlier, and the abstract concept of the baby has too long to balloon into something frightening. Start much later, and the child has almost no time to absorb the information before the baby is physically present.

Age shapes how these conversations go. A 20-month-old needs simple, repeated sentences: "A baby is growing in Mama's belly. The baby will come and live with us." A 3-year-old can handle more: the baby cries a great deal, cannot walk, will sleep in its own space, and will need help from the big sibling. A 4-year-old can track a rough timeline when it is pegged to something concrete: "The baby comes around the time school starts again."

One consistent mistake is treating preparation as a single conversation. It is a steady trickle of small accurate facts offered in calm moments, perhaps twice a week, so that by the time the baby arrives the toddler has heard the key ideas twenty or thirty times. Familiarity is the point. A fact heard thirty times feels like something the child already knows rather than news arriving at the worst possible moment.

What Do Toddlers Actually Understand About a New Baby?

Toddlers understand far less about babies than adults assume, and far more about fairness and attention than adults remember. A child under 3 has no working model of what a newborn means in practice: the crying, the feeding every two hours, the physical helplessness. When parents say "a baby is coming," many toddlers picture something closer to a playmate who can immediately run and share toys.

The correction is specific. Tell the child that the baby will not be able to talk, walk, or play for a long time. Tell them the baby will need to be held a great deal and that this will look like it takes something from them, but the child's place in the family does not shrink because there is more family. Show photographs of what a newborn actually looks like. Read books together that depict real scenes: a parent occupied with a fussing baby while the older child waits, the baby sleeping when the toddler wants company.

What toddlers grasp immediately, by contrast, is role. They know when they have a job and when they do not. Assigning a concrete sibling role before the birth gives the child something to be rather than something to endure. "You will be the one who shows the baby our house." "You are the one who knows where the good spots to sleep are." A child who has been assigned a real job will look for that job, not for ways to feel displaced.

Which Phrases Actually Help You Prepare a Toddler for a New Sibling?

Language matters because toddlers narrate their experience in the words they have been given. When no words exist for the feelings that come with a new sibling, a child uses actions instead: hitting, biting, regressing, refusing. Scripts offered in advance are the alternative to those behaviors.

Start with "I feel jealous right now." Say this aloud yourself, in ordinary moments, so the word lands in the child's vocabulary before they need it in a hard one. Then teach "I need some time with you" explicitly, so the child has a way to ask directly rather than acting out to capture attention. Both sentences give the child a verbal tool for a feeling that would otherwise come out sideways.

The sentence you yourself will use most deserves practice before the birth: "The baby needs me right now. I will be with you in a few minutes." A toddler who has heard these words before hears them as a known sentence, not an abandonment.

Books that show real sibling dynamics cement this language before it is needed. A New Baby in Our Home uses actual photographs of families working through this adjustment, which makes the abstract concrete in a way illustrated fiction often cannot. Reading it together in the weeks before the birth lets a toddler see their own situation reflected before they are living it.

How Do You Prepare Your Toddler's Sense of Place in the Family?

The deepest fear for a toddler is not the baby. It is the loss of position. Every preparation strategy works best when it addresses this fear directly. The child needs to know, in plain terms, that the love in this family does not divide when it expands. That sentence sounds obvious to an adult and is entirely non-obvious to a 2-year-old.

Concrete actions carry more weight than reassurance alone. Keep one ritual per day unchanged: a bath routine, a particular song at bedtime, a standing joke that belongs only to the two of you. These are proof, given nightly, that the child's relationship with you remains intact. Research on sibling adjustment from the 1980s, including Judy Dunn's studies at Cambridge, found that the quality of the firstborn's relationship with a parent in the first months after a new birth predicted the quality of the sibling relationship years later. The investment in small rituals returns over time.

Toddlers also respond to being made agents in the change rather than recipients of it. Involve the child in setting up the baby's sleep area. Let them choose a small gift to present the baby when it arrives. Pack a special bag for the older child during the hospital stay, assembled together in advance. Agency reduces panic. A child who helped choose the baby's blanket has a stake in the baby's arrival.

The parent who started this preparation twelve weeks out, who read the same books over and over and kept the bedtime song unchanged: that parent does not need to be perfect in the exhausting first weeks. The groundwork holds even when the execution is messy.

FAQ

How early should I tell my toddler about a new sibling?

For most children ages 18 months to 4 years, starting the conversation 12 to 16 weeks before the due date gives enough time to absorb the information without an overly long stretch of anxious waiting. Younger toddlers benefit from simpler, shorter conversations repeated more often. A child of 2 gains the most from hearing the same core facts twice a week until the birth, so the idea feels familiar rather than sudden when the baby arrives.

What if my toddler regresses after the new sibling comes home?

Regression is normal and temporary. A toddler who resumes thumb-sucking, asks for a bottle, or needs nighttime help again is doing exactly what stressed children do: reaching for earlier comfort strategies. Responding with matter-of-fact warmth rather than correction shortens the regression. The behavior almost always resolves within six to eight weeks as the new routine settles and the child confirms their place is secure.

How do I handle my toddler hitting or pushing the new baby?

Separate first, explain second, assign a job third. Remove the child from the baby's space without escalating the emotional charge. Then name what happened without shaming: "You pushed the baby. That is not safe." Then redirect to the sibling role: "Your job is to show the baby how we are gentle. Show me how you do that." Repeating this sequence consistently, rather than only stopping the behavior, gives the child a concrete alternative to replace it with.
