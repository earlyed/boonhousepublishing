Toddler Hits When Frustrated: How to Stop It Before Anyone Gets Hurt

When your toddler hits when frustrated, here's how to stop it: the mechanism, exact scripts, and what to practice between incidents.

By BoonHouse Publishing · 2026-07-18

Maya, 28 months, wanted the red cup. It was Tuesday morning snack, and her older brother had the red cup. By the time their mom crossed the kitchen, Maya's open hand had already connected with his arm, and two children were crying.

When a toddler hits when frustrated, the behavior is predictable, not personal. Children between 18 and 36 months feel fully adult-scale emotions but have a vocabulary of perhaps 50 to 300 words. Hitting fills the gap. The fix has two parts: a calm, brief interruption in the moment, and a replacement skill practiced during peaceful times between incidents.

Why Does a Toddler Hit When Frustrated?

The prefrontal cortex, the region of the brain that governs impulse control and verbal problem-solving, is not structurally mature until the mid-twenties. In a 2-year-old, it is barely online. Developmental psychologist Dr. Ross Greene, whose 2014 work on collaborative problem-solving established the concept of lagging executive skills, argued that children who cannot produce expected behavior under stress are not choosing to misbehave. Their brains cannot yet deliver what adults are asking for in that moment.

What you can actually observe at your kitchen table is that your child was completely calm, then something was taken away or blocked or refused, and within about two seconds they were hitting, with no visible pause between the frustration arriving and the hand moving. That two-second window is the whole story.

Toddlers also hit because it works. When your child hits, the situation changes immediately: someone cries, someone gives the toy back, an adult arrives. From a 2-year-old's perspective, hitting is remarkably effective communication. Replacing it means making hitting reliably ineffective while making words reliably effective. That is the full project, and it takes weeks of consistent follow-through.

What Should You Do When Your Toddler Hits?

Speed and calm are the two non-negotiables. Move in quickly, get to eye level, and use the fewest words possible.

The script: “Hands down. Hitting hurts. You can say ‘I want that.’”

Say those three sentences and nothing else. Do not explain that feelings are okay but hitting is not, because a dysregulated 2-year-old cannot process a compound emotional lesson while flooded. The fewer words you use, the more likely one of them will land.

Then give the frustration a label and a door out: “You're so mad. Tell me mad.” If your child can say “mad” or even grunt in response, you have started the replacement pathway in real time.

The fallback, for when hitting continues after the first script: remove your child from the situation. Not as a punishment with a timer, but as a reset. “We're going to take a break.” Hold them gently if they allow it. Wait for the body to settle, which you can see when breathing slows and muscles soften, then try once more: “Ready to try again?”

One thing most parents do that accidentally extends hitting episodes is keep talking. Verbal escalation from a parent signals high emotional stakes to a toddler, which raises their arousal and makes hitting more likely. The moment you feel yourself explaining at length, stop. Silence plus proximity works better than any sentence you could add.

How to Stop Toddler Hitting: What to Practice Between Incidents

Replacement skills cannot be taught in the moment of hitting. They have to be installed during calm time and retrieved under pressure later. This is why “we don't hit, we use our words” said mid-meltdown almost never produces lasting change. The phrase is true, but a flooded brain cannot learn from it.

What actually works is five minutes of daily practice when nothing is wrong.

Read books together that name feelings explicitly. When your toddler sees a frustrated character, ask: “What does she feel? What could she do?” The answer matters less than the practice of running that circuit: feeling recognized, alternative action chosen. Before I Hit: A Montessori Story About Big Feelings and Safe Choices walks toddlers through exactly that loop in language and imagery a 2-year-old can absorb.

Role-play the frustrating moment before it happens again. “What if your brother had your cup? Show me what you could do.” Toddlers who rehearse a replacement behavior during calm conditions retrieve it faster when emotion runs high. The research on impulse control in early childhood consistently shows that repetition across varied low-stakes situations is what builds the neural pathway, not a single correction in the moment of crisis.

Name your own frustration out loud, briefly: “I burned the toast and I'm frustrated. I'm going to take a breath.” You are narrating the mechanism in real time. Children this age learn regulation primarily by watching a regulated adult handle a small frustration without hitting something.

What Gets Harder Before It Gets Better

The first two weeks of consistent redirection are often worse than before you started. Your child is testing whether the new response is real, and hitting may increase slightly in frequency before it drops. Parents who stop during this window often conclude the approach does not work, when it has simply not had enough time yet.

You will also get inconsistent. You will be tired or distracted, and you will let one incident go without the script, or you will lecture instead of keeping it brief. That inconsistency is not failure. Return to the script the next time. Consistency across a month matters more than perfection on any given afternoon.

The child who stops hitting reliably by age 3 usually still has hitting incidents at 2 years and 4 months. Progress is not a straight line, and progress that slow can feel invisible until you look back across six weeks.

Maya's mom used the two-sentence script every single time and stopped herself from adding explanations. Three weeks in, Maya started saying “mad” unprompted when her brother took something. Not every time. About one time in four. That is what progress looks like at 28 months.

FAQ

Is it normal for a 2-year-old to hit when frustrated?

Yes, entirely normal. Between 18 and 36 months, most toddlers hit at some point because their emotional intensity outpaces their vocabulary. Research on early childhood behavior consistently finds that hitting peaks in the second year of life and declines as language expands. It is a developmental phase, not a sign of aggression disorder or poor parenting.

How long does it take to stop a toddler from hitting?

Expect four to eight weeks of consistent redirection before you see reliable change. Some children shift faster when they have enough language to use words instead; others take longer. The key variable is consistency: the more reliably hitting produces a calm, brief response and words produce results, the faster the behavior changes.

What should I do if my toddler hits me, not a sibling?

The same script applies: “Hands down. Hitting hurts. You can say ‘I want help.’” Then pause contact briefly. Sit back, look away for five seconds, then return. Over time your toddler learns that hitting ends connection rather than producing it, which is a natural consequence that registers even at this age without lectures or timeouts.
