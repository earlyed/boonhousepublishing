Sleep Regression at 18 Months: What to Expect and What to Do

The 18-month sleep regression explained: what to expect, how long it lasts, and what to do to help your toddler sleep through the night again.

By BoonHouse Publishing · 2026-08-15

The 18-month sleep regression is a predictable disruption in toddler sleep that appears, in most children, between 15 and 20 months of age. A toddler who slept reliably begins waking at night, refusing naps, or turning bedtime into a prolonged battle. This is a neurological event. The toddler brain at 18 months is processing a flood of new language, new physical capability, and a dawning sense of personal autonomy, and sleep is where the brain consolidates all of it. The disruption is the consolidation.

Parents searching for what to expect from this phase will find the symptoms consistent enough to recognize within the first night or two. What varies is how long it lasts and what makes it better. Both questions have specific, research-grounded answers.

What Causes the 18-Month Sleep Regression?

Several developmental processes converge around 18 months, and any one of them would disrupt sleep. Together, they reliably do.

Language is the most significant driver. Between 16 and 24 months, most toddlers experience their first major vocabulary burst, and a child who had 10 words may suddenly acquire 50. The brain lays down and reinforces neural pathways during slow-wave sleep, which means lighter sleep cycles and more wakings as new language circuitry is built.

Alongside language, motor integration continues during sleep. Walking has become confident enough that running and climbing are now the frontier, and the nervous system keeps working through these skills overnight. Some toddlers thrash, talk in their sleep, or call out as motor sequences replay.

A third driver is the emergence of separation awareness. At 18 months, object permanence is fully established, and with it comes the understanding that a parent who is out of sight is somewhere else. This is the neurological basis of separation anxiety, and it peaks in this window. Bedtime is a separation, and the toddler brain is now fully capable of registering that fact and objecting to it.

Finally, the word "no" becomes a tool rather than just a sound. A child discovering their own will tests every limit, including the one called sleep.

What Should Parents Expect from the 18-Month Sleep Regression?

The pattern is consistent enough to anticipate symptom by symptom.

Night wakings return. A toddler who was sleeping 10 to 11 hours straight may begin waking once, twice, or three times per night, calling out or appearing at the bedroom door. The wakings often concentrate in the first two hours after sleep onset or in the early morning hours before the usual wake time.

Nap resistance spikes. The 18-month period is when many toddlers show new resistance to their single afternoon nap. They may take 40 minutes to settle, refuse entirely for two or three days, then sleep hard the following afternoon. This inconsistency is a symptom of the regression. Most children still need one afternoon nap until somewhere between 2.5 and 3 years of age.

Bedtime refusal intensifies. Toddlers who were calm at bedtime may suddenly cry, cling, or invent reasons to stay awake. The drive toward autonomy and the pull of separation anxiety move in the same direction here, and together they are loud.

Daytime mood shifts arrive alongside the sleep disruption. A sleep-deprived 18-month-old shows more meltdowns, lower frustration tolerance, and more difficulty with transitions. Fragmented sleep produces exactly this pattern. The child is showing the cost.

A 2018 review published in Sleep Medicine Reviews confirmed that the period from 15 to 18 months shows a statistically significant increase in reported night wakings across the majority of toddlers studied, with a median regression duration of 3 to 6 weeks before sleep consolidates again in children whose caregivers maintained consistent bedtime routines.

What Actually Helps: Strategies to Use During the 18-Month Sleep Regression

The work during a regression is to hold the structure steady. When the regression ends, the child needs a familiar routine to return to.

Keep the routine anchor points fixed. Bedtime routine length, order, and time should stay the same even when the child resists. Consistency gives the nervous system a familiar sequence to follow when everything else feels uncertain. Twenty minutes of the same steps, same room, same ending, every night.

Respond briefly, then return. For night wakings, a short calm reassurance followed by leaving the room teaches the child that you are present but nighttime is for sleeping. Staying long or bringing the child into the parental bed teaches the opposite lesson and extends the regression. Brief presence, repeated consistently, is the mechanism.

Hold the nap. Even when resistance is high, keep the nap opportunity in the schedule. A quiet rest period in the same location at the same time sends the signal the body has already learned. The nap often returns within 7 to 10 days if the structure is maintained.

Practice separations during the day. A child who has repeated experiences of you leaving and returning, in low-stakes daytime situations, builds confidence that the nighttime separation is also survivable. Five minutes in the backyard while they watch from the window. A brief errand with a consistent, warm reunion. The warmth of your return matters as much as the fact of your leaving.

Name the experience for them. Toddlers at 18 months understand far more language than they can produce. "You want Mama. Mama is sleeping in the next room. Mama will come when it is morning" is comprehensible and genuinely calming in a way that silence or vague reassurance often is not.

How Long Does the 18-Month Sleep Regression Last?

For most toddlers, the regression improves measurably within 3 to 6 weeks when the strategies above are applied consistently. Without a consistent response, it can stretch longer. An inconsistent response teaches the child that pushing past the limit sometimes works, which extends the regression beyond its neurological cause.

Expect the recovery to be gradual rather than sudden. Night wakings space out before they disappear. The child resettles faster before they stop waking. Nap resistance eases before the nap snaps back to its old length. A week of better sleep followed by one difficult night is still progress.

Signs the regression is ending: wakings become shorter, daytime mood improves noticeably, and bedtime resistance drops. These signs usually arrive together over the course of a week, and they tend to be unmistakable after the preceding weeks.

If sleep remains significantly disrupted beyond 8 weeks, or if the child shows signs of respiratory distress during sleep, frequent loud snoring, very early morning waking alongside weight loss, or extreme daytime drowsiness, discuss it with a pediatrician. These can indicate something separate from the developmental regression.

The regression passes. The mechanism that causes it is time-limited. Once the brain has processed what it needs to process, sleep reorganizes on its own.

FAQ

Is the 18-month sleep regression the same as separation anxiety?

They overlap but are not identical. The sleep regression has multiple causes, with the language and motor developmental surge as its primary drivers. Separation anxiety is one contributor to bedtime resistance and night waking during this period, but a toddler can experience the full regression without severe separation anxiety, and some children show significant separation anxiety without the classic regression pattern.

Should I drop the nap if my 18-month-old is fighting it?

Not yet. Nap resistance at 18 months is almost always a temporary symptom of the regression. Most toddlers need one afternoon nap until somewhere between 2.5 and 3 years. Keep the nap opportunity in place and maintain the schedule consistently for at least two to three weeks before reconsidering the timing.

My toddler was sleep trained. Why is the regression happening now?

Sleep training teaches a skill, and that skill is retained. But it cannot prevent developmental disruptions. The 18-month regression is driven by brain changes that happen regardless of prior sleep training. A previously sleep-trained child typically returns to their baseline faster once the regression ends, because the underlying skill is still there.
