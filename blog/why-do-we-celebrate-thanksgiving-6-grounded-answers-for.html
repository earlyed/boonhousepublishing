Why Do We Celebrate Thanksgiving? 6 Grounded Answers for Every Age

Why do we celebrate Thanksgiving? Six honest, age-right answers covering the 1621 history, gratitude science, and Native perspectives.

By BoonHouse Publishing · 2026-06-11

Thanksgiving is rooted in a three-day harvest feast at Plymouth Colony in autumn 1621, shared between 53 Pilgrim survivors and approximately 90 Wampanoag men. That is the documented origin. Abraham Lincoln made it a federal holiday in 1863, during the Civil War, framing it as a time for national gratitude despite catastrophic loss. The holiday we celebrate today carries both of those moments forward, layered with 400 years of American meaning: family gathering, ritual food, and honest conversation about whose story gets told in the history books.

When a child asks why we celebrate Thanksgiving, the question almost always has a specific subtext. A 4-year-old wants to know why there is pie and why everyone is coming over. A 7-year-old heard something at school that contradicts what grandma says. A teenager wants to know whether the holiday is worth keeping. Each version deserves a real answer. This list gives you six grounded, honest answers tied to the moments they actually come up, with the exact words to use.

1. Because a Harvest Feast Actually Happened in 1621

When to use this: A child ages 3 to 6 asks what Thanksgiving is, or asks why the family has a special dinner.

What to say: "A long time ago, some families moved to a new place where they didn't know how to farm. A group of people called the Wampanoag taught them how to grow food. When the harvest came in, everyone celebrated together for three days. We still celebrate every year to remember that, and to say thank you for what we have."

This answer is accurate and complete for young children. It names the Wampanoag from the start, which matters. Children under 7 think in events and objects, so the feast is the right anchor. This is the correct size of the story for a 4-year-old brain, a real answer rather than a deflection.

If a child asks follow-up questions about who the Wampanoag are today, the honest answer is that they still exist: the Wampanoag Nation is based in southeastern Massachusetts and has thousands of enrolled members. That fact belongs in the first conversation, not saved for later.

2. Because Gratitude Is a Skill That Takes Practice

When to use this: A child resists the "everyone say what you're grateful for" ritual at the table, or goes silent when asked.

What to say: "You don't have to feel grateful to say thank you. Sometimes the words come first and the feeling follows. What's one small thing today that went okay, even something tiny?"

A 2015 study published in the journal Emotion found that children who verbalize gratitude regularly, even briefly and specifically, develop measurably stronger social bonds over time than children who are simply prompted to feel it. The specificity of the prompt matters more than the grandeur of the answer. A child who says "the mashed potatoes were good" has done the exercise correctly.

One true, small sentence from each person at the table does more than the most eloquent speech from one. Thanksgiving works when it stops being a performance and becomes a practice. Lowering the stakes is the mechanism that makes the whole ritual function.

Why Do We Celebrate Thanksgiving When the History Is Complicated?

When to use this: A child ages 7 to 12 reports that Thanksgiving is harmful, asks why some families don't celebrate, or comes home with a version of the history that contradicts what they have heard at home.

What to say: "The Wampanoag people were already living here when the Pilgrims arrived. They helped the Pilgrims survive. The 1621 feast really happened. The years after were very hard for Native people. Some Native families mark this day as a time of mourning instead. Both responses come from the same true history. We can observe Thanksgiving and still know the story is not simple."

Children who hear this version do not abandon the holiday. They hold it more honestly, which is a form of respect for everyone in the story. The goal is accuracy, and children can handle accuracy. A child who learns the complicated version of a holiday is given tools to think about it, which turns out to be more useful than being shielded from the parts that require thought.

4. Because Family Traditions Are How Belonging Gets Built

When to use this: A child asks why you do the same things every year, or why the food and the guests never change.

What to say: "When we do the same things together each year, we're building something. The food, the people, the same table: those things say this is who we are, and you belong here. The repetition is the point."

Family studies research from the 1990s and 2000s shows consistently that children raised in households with stable rituals carry a stronger sense of identity into adolescence, with lower rates of anxiety than children raised without them. Children often feel Thanksgiving before they understand it, because the body registers belonging before the mind can name it. The annual repetition is the mechanism, not an accident of habit.

What Do We Actually Celebrate When We Celebrate Thanksgiving?

When to use this: The standard gratitude round at the table feels flat, or someone at the table says they don't feel grateful this year.

What to say: "Let's name one hard thing from this year, and one thing that happened because someone helped us. Both of those belong at Thanksgiving."

This prompt opens the table to real life. When adults name something genuinely hard alongside something they value, children learn that Thanksgiving holds the full year, not a curated summary of what went well. The 1621 feast followed a year of illness and death among the Pilgrim colonists. The celebration came after the difficulty, because of it. That is what gratitude at the table can mean: naming the year as it actually was.

6. Because Gratitude Looks Different Across Cultures and Languages

When to use this: A child asks whether other countries celebrate Thanksgiving, or whether saying thank you is an American idea.

What to say: "Most cultures have a harvest celebration or a day set aside for gratitude. Canada observes Thanksgiving in October. Japan has a national Labor Thanksgiving Day in November. Korea has Chuseok in autumn. Different words, different foods, same human impulse: stop, notice what you have, say it out loud."

Gratitude is not an American invention, and connecting Thanksgiving to that global pattern removes the American-exceptionalism weight without diminishing the holiday. The American version is one named occasion among many that does the same work across different cultures and calendars. FAQ: Why Do We Celebrate Thanksgiving?

What is the real historical reason we celebrate Thanksgiving in the United States?

The federal holiday was established by Abraham Lincoln in 1863, during the Civil War, shaped in part by 17 years of advocacy from the writer Sarah Josepha Hale. The cultural origin is the 1621 harvest feast at Plymouth Colony, shared between Pilgrim colonists and the Wampanoag people. The holiday today carries both founding moments: the 1621 feast as origin story, and Lincoln's 1863 proclamation as the legal and emotional frame that made it national. Both belong in the answer.

How do you explain Thanksgiving to a child without glossing over the Native American history?

Name the Wampanoag by name from the beginning, not as a footnote. Give children the facts in sequence: the Wampanoag were there first, they taught the colonists to farm, the feast happened, and the years that followed brought harm to Native communities across the continent. Children as young as 6 can hold two truths about one event: that something was shared, and that something was also taken. Both are part of understanding why we celebrate Thanksgiving.

Why do some Native American families not celebrate Thanksgiving?

The United American Indians of New England have held a National Day of Mourning at Plymouth, Massachusetts since 1970, marking what the holiday represents for Native communities who lost land, language, and lives in the colonial period. This is an honest accounting of what the holiday's history contains. Knowing it is part of understanding what Thanksgiving means, for everyone who observes it.
